Personal project · Design complete, build starting

Desktop CNC Lathe

A small CNC lathe I designed from scratch in SolidWorks to take real cuts in steel for about $1,000. The mini lathes that fit the budget have about 2" of swing over the cross slide, converting one to CNC costs as much as building my own, and nobody sells a ready made conversion kit for them anymore. So I designed one.

  • A bed without machining one. A self leveling epoxy deck is poured across a steel tube base. The linear rails and a Sieg C2 headstock sit on that pour, so they come out coplanar.
  • Decisions from the math. Radial stiffness and motor torque set nearly every choice. Going from one bearing block per rail to two cut the estimated tool deflection at the cross slide from about 30 µm to about 2 µm, for $15 to $30.
  • Staged build. v0.5 runs NEMA 17 steppers and a 3/4 hp three phase spindle motor on a VFD. v1 swaps in NEMA 23s on the same frame, so the upgrade is a swap, not a rebuild.
  • Checked before cutting metal. The fully mated assembly drives both slides and the spindle through their full travel against the real hard stops, with interference checks at every corner.

Formula SAE Aerodynamics

I'm on the aero team for UD's Formula SAE car, helping design, manufacture and test the aerodynamic components for the 2027 car. The whole car lives in one shared drive, with every part, the subsystem assemblies, test assemblies and the full car assembly, and we work across all of it.

  • Designed to the process. The parts have to be designed for carbon fiber composite manufacturing: resin transfer molding in matched die molds or, more often, injection compression molding, which is new for the team this year. At UD, Formula SAE can count as the senior design project, so I'm learning alongside the senior aero team leads while helping with the design, manufacturing, adjustments and redesigns.
  • Validating it for real. I'm building a scale test bed version of the car carrying our actual aero designs, so we can test them in a real wind tunnel and see how the designs and the CFD hold up in the real world.
  • Tested until it fails or it's proven. At our near weekly drive days, everything that's been designed, changed, added or removed gets tested until it fails or we have hours of data to validate it. I helped manufacture new tie rods that went through that testing.

UD machine shop · Fall 2026 · In use

Aluminum Lathe Soft Jaws

I reverse engineered these from an older set of jaws, then drew, machined and installed them on the shop's lathe chuck. The full set is in use on the shop lathe for extra wide parts.

  • A real fit problem. The chuck's hardened steel interface measures .4999". Steel jaws press fit at exactly .500", but that doesn't work once one side is softer aluminum. I measured it, checked with the shop's master machinist, and revised the key on the drawing of record to .490", nominal and still tight enough to lock in place.
  • Dimensioned for the setup. The part is machined upside down, so every dimension is called from the corner that's X/Y zero in that orientation. The operator reads coordinates straight off the print instead of chaining dimensions and stacking up error.

Melissa & Doug sponsored project · UD · Spring 2026

The Mighty Mower

A working push mower toy for kids: a motor driven blade that actually picks things up into a catch tray. It had to meet the sponsor's requirements, the course requirements, and the toy safety standards ASTM F963 and ISO 8124. Fitting a functional design inside all three was the real challenge. I carried the CAD, electronics and firmware for the team.

  • Process and material, part by part. Laser cut MDF for the body, wood for the wheels and handles, and 3D printed PETG for the mounts and catch tray.
  • A blade that can't hurt a kid. The blade is printed in nylon filament. At a few millimeters thick it's flexible enough to give way if a kid's finger gets in there instead of cutting, and the motor has so little torque that it stalls first anyway.
  • Electronics. Arduino Mega, a DC motor on an L9110 driver, a DFPlayer audio module, and an LED and button.

Personal project · In progress

Two Axis Pan and Tilt Mechanism

A two axis turret I'm designing. Both axes use the same motor, pinion, gear ratio and center distance, so there's one drive module to design and test, and the prototype mirrors the final layout so scaling up is a parts swap, not a redesign. Printed parts carry the geometry and steel carries the loads.

On the bench I got first motion on a NEMA 17 and traced a shudder in place fault to a motor cable whose pinout didn't match the driver.

Motivation

Why defense

Field faster, build more

Growing up, I went to airshows every summer, and watching the F-22 fly made me think we had to be years ahead of everyone. It looked like something from the future. I still love that kind of engineering, but being the most advanced no longer buys the edge it used to. Cheap drones, loitering munitions and missiles fielded by the thousands can overwhelm a handful of exquisite systems, and AI and hypersonics are changing combat as much as tanks and machine guns once did.

I think that means designing differently: systems that can be fielded much faster, built at real volume, and made for specific problems instead of one platform stretched to cover everything at a production rate of a few a year. It takes engineers who understand how things get made and tested, not just how they're designed, and it takes building them here again.

That's the work I want to do: making sure the men and women who serve always have the edge over our adversaries.
